### Cover letter (optional but recommended)

Dear medRxiv editors,

I am submitting this manuscript, "Wearable Photoplethysmography Signal-Quality Methods Disagree at Scale: A Multi-Baseline Audit of the WESAD Dataset," for pre-print posting.

The paper reports an empirical finding on the public WESAD wearable-physiology benchmark (n = 15 subjects, 6,585 5-second windows): three independently developed published wrist-PPG signal-quality methods (Orphanidou 2015, Sukor 2011, Elgendi 2016) collectively reject 44.6% of windows accepted by a representative in-house threshold. The three published methods disagree with each other on the gray area (median pairwise Cohen's kappa = -0.20) but converge on the consensus rejection set. Single-threshold recalibration at n = 15 does not recover agreement, and per-subject heterogeneity in cross-modality HR disagreement spans a four-fold range across subjects. The finding replicates on a second public benchmark (PPG-DaLiA, n = 15, 18,781 windows; same wrist-PPG hardware but ambulatory activity paradigm rather than stress paradigm): consensus rejection 43.1%, median pairwise kappa across published methods -0.20, kappa = 0.000 against the in-house threshold across all three published methods. The implication for wearable-AI research practice is that single-method SQI reporting is insufficient; multi-baseline auditing should be standard supplementary material.

The audit pipeline used in this study is openly available as biomedical-signal-forensics-lab (v0.16.2, MIT licensed, 235 passing tests across Python 3.11 and 3.12). The associated software paper has been simultaneously submitted to the Journal of Open Source Software; this medRxiv pre-print is the methodology and findings paper, the JOSS submission is the software paper for the underlying toolkit.

The work declares no external funding and no competing interests. The complete audit on both datasets is reproducible end-to-end from a fixed seed in under five minutes on a laptop CPU.

## Possible screening rejections to anticipate

medRxiv's screening team checks for:

- Adequate methodological detail (yours has methods section with subsections 2.1-2.9)
- Ethics statement (present)
- Clear data and code availability (present, with three independent locations)
- Reasonable scope (a methodology paper with real-data validation is in scope)
- No personal patient data (n/a, secondary analysis)
- Not a duplicate submission (the JOSS software paper is a different paper about the software; disclose in cover letter)

Common gotchas that get bounced:

- Missing affiliation for the author (yours is present)
- Missing competing-interests declaration (yours is present)
- Cover letter that overstates clinical claims (yours is methodological, no clinical claims)
- Suggesting that the paper is a clinical trial (it is not, and yours correctly says so)

Low probability of screening rejection. If it happens, the rejection email will say why and you can revise and resubmit.
